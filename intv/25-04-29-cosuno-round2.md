## What This Round Is Testing

The head of product is not evaluating your technical stack. They are evaluating whether you are the kind of person who will:

1. **Bring ideas, not wait to be told what to build** — Cosuno is a startup at ~85 people; they don't have bandwidth to hand-hold. They want someone who reads the business and identifies problems independently.
2. **Own outcomes, not tasks** — the difference between "I built the workflow" and "I reduced the sales team's manual data entry by 8 hours per week and tracked it for three months."
3. **Understand the product and business context** — automation that doesn't connect to what Cosuno actually sells and how they make money isn't interesting to a product leader.
4. **Be motivated for the right reasons** — they're a Series B startup, flat hierarchy, direct access to founders. They want someone energised by that environment, not just looking for a stable job.

This is essentially a "would I want to work with this person and could they operate independently?" interview.

### "Tell me about a time you automated something with real impact."

**Option A — n8n LinkedIn outreach flow:**
"I built an end-to-end LinkedIn prospecting and outreach automation using n8n. The brief was: reduce the manual time a sales team spent on lead research and first-touch sequencing. **Option B — Insurance analytics pipeline:**
"For a global insurance firm I built an LLM pipeline that analysed 50,000+ customer service conversations to measure agent adherence to quality guidelines. Before that, quality managers were manually reviewing a sample of maybe 200 conversations per quarter. The automation gave them coverage across the full dataset with confidence scores, flagged outliers for human review, and ran via BigQuery and Looker as a regular report. The QA function went from sampling to systematic measurement. The impact was both efficiency (fewer manual hours) and quality (issues they'd never have found in a sample)."

### "This is a new role — how would you prioritise when everything feels urgent?"

"New roles in growing companies always have that tension — everyone has a problem they think is most important. My approach is to score ruthlessly on two variables: data readiness and frequency × people affected. Data readiness because a technically interesting automation that requires six months of data cleanup first isn't a quick win. Frequency × people because a two-hour-per-week task affecting ten people is twenty hours per week — that's worth attacking even if it's boring. Quick wins first, deliberately, to build the trust and mandate for harder projects."

### "What's your product sense like? How do you think about user adoption?"

"An automation that nobody uses is indistinguishable from one that doesn't exist. I approach internal tools the same way a product manager approaches a feature: identify the user, understand the job they're trying to do, build the minimum that solves it, measure whether they actually use it.

The failure mode I try to avoid is building something technically impressive that requires the team to change their workflow to accommodate it. The automation has to fit into how they already work — in Slack, in Salesforce, in their existing rhythm — not ask them to learn a new tool. That usually means the first version is simpler than I'd want it to be, but it's the one that gets adopted."

### "How do you handle people who are resistant to change or to using AI?"

**Answer (STAR):**
"The first thing I try to understand is what kind of resistance it is — because they're different problems. There's scepticism ('I don't think this will work'), there's anxiety ('I'm worried this replaces me'), and there's legitimate pushback ('this specific tool isn't right for what I actually do'). Treating all three the same way doesn't work.

For scepticism, the only thing that works is evidence in their domain. Not a generic AI demo — showing them an output on a piece of work they recognise. At the Canadian engineering firm, one of the project managers was openly dismissive until I ran the tool on one of his actual reports and he could see the difference himself. That took ten minutes. A 45-minute training session wouldn't have moved him.

For anxiety — and this comes up more than people admit — I find being direct about it is better than talking around it. If someone is worried their job is at risk, acknowledging that directly and being honest about what the tool actually does (speeds up specific tasks, doesn't replace judgment) lands better than pretending the concern isn't there. People can tell when they're being managed.

For legitimate pushback, I genuinely listen. Sometimes resistance is correct. If someone tells me the automation doesn't fit their workflow, that's useful information — either the tool needs adjusting or it's the wrong problem to automate. I'd rather find that out from a sceptic early than discover it after rollout.

The common thread: I don't try to overcome resistance through enthusiasm or pressure. I try to make the right answer obvious through direct, small demonstrations on real work."

It also means I'm accountable for outcomes, not activities — I'm not done when the workflow is built, I'm done when it's been proven to work or proven not to, and I've documented what I learned. In a startup context it also means I'm comfortable deciding what's in scope and what isn't, and flagging trade-offs to the founders rather than escalating every decision."

### "What motivates you about AI specifically?"

"The leverage. A well-designed automation doesn't just save time — it changes what's possible. When a team stops spending four hours a week on a manual task, they don't just get four hours back; they get cognitive space. They start thinking about things they couldn't afford to think about before. That's the kind of impact I find motivating — not the engineering itself but what the engineering enables.

The reason I'm excited about AI specifically now, versus earlier automation tools, is that the class of problems you can solve has expanded dramatically. Five years ago, automating 'find all the contractors who've worked on a project of this type in this region' was a database query. Automating 'summarise the risk profile of this bid based on contractor history' requires the kind of reasoning LLMs are now capable of. That's a new category of possible."

## 30-60-90 Day Plan

*Use this for the challenge round or if any interviewer asks "how would you approach this role?" — it's also the honest answer to the "first 90 days" question.*

### Days 1–30: Understand before building

**Goal:** Know the org well enough to prioritise with confidence, not instinct.

**Actions:**
- One-on-one with each department head (Sales, Finance, Ops, Product, potentially CS). Ask two questions only: "What do you do repeatedly that you wish you didn't?" and "What do you always mean to do but never get to?"
- Shadow one person in each team for a half-day — watch their actual workflow, not a description of it. The gap between what people say they do and what they actually do is where automation opportunity lives.
- Audit the current tool stack: which systems actually connect to each other? Where is data manually moved between tools? Document the connective tissue and the gaps.
- Identify the three highest-ROI targets using: frequency × people affected × data readiness. Present the prioritised list to Christoph and Max and validate before building anything.

**Governance deliverable:**
- Produce an **Automation Opportunity Register** — a living Notion table with: problem description, affected team, estimated hours saved per week, data readiness score, effort estimate, status. This becomes the single source of truth for what's in flight and what's next. Share it with CEO and CTO from day one — no surprises, full visibility.

---

### Days 31–60: First builds, instrumented from the start

**Goal:** Ship two automations into production and prove the measurement model works.

**Target #1 (Week 5–6): Sales CRM enrichment**
- Automate lead research + personalised first-touch sequencing via n8n → Salesforce → Salesloft
- Baseline measurement taken in week 5: how many minutes per lead does the current process take?
- Instrumentation: n8n execution logs + a lightweight Notion tracker showing runs/week and errors/week
- 30-day review with Sales: confirm/reject the time-saving hypothesis. If it's working, expand scope. If not, understand why and iterate.

**Target #2 (Week 7–8): Quick win for a second team**
- Chosen from the Opportunity Register based on which team has the cleanest data and clearest problem. Likely Finance (invoice processing or expense categorisation) or Product (support ticket clustering).
- Same instrumentation model: baseline → build → measure.

**Governance deliverable:**
- For each automation: a one-page **Hypothesis Card** before build (what we're solving, what success looks like in numbers, measurement window) and a **Retrospective Note** at 30 days (what we built, what we expected, what happened, what we'd change). These go into a shared folder — the retrospectives compound into a pattern library over time.

---

### Days 61–90: Platform thinking and the AI champion model

**Goal:** Move from individual automations to a system that scales and that the org can grow into.

**Actions:**
- Build the **data connectivity layer** — a single integration setup (Slack, Salesforce, email) that future automations can plug into without rebuilding from scratch. This is the plumbing that makes everything after it faster.
- Set up **n8n properly**: hosted (not local), with credential management, error alerting piped to Slack, and a Git export script for version control. This is invisible infrastructure that prevents incidents at 3am.
- Launch the **AI champion programme** — identify one person in each department who is curious about AI and brief them on: how to bring an automation request to this team, what makes a good candidate problem, what they can expect back. This is a lightweight process, not a training programme. The goal is to create a pull, not a push.

**Governance deliverable:**
- Monthly **Automation Review** with CEO and CTO (30 minutes): Opportunity Register update, active workflows health check (runs, errors, feedback), experiments confirmed/killed, next priorities agreed. This is the forcing function that keeps the work aligned with company priorities and visible to founders.
- **Experiment Registry** — by day 90 there should be 4–6 completed experiments. The registry is: name, hypothesis, result, learning. One paragraph each. This is the institutional memory of what works at Cosuno specifically.

### Governance Summary

| Layer | What | Cadence |
|---|---|---|
| Automation Opportunity Register | All known problems, scored and prioritised | Live (updated continuously) |
| Hypothesis Card | Pre-build contract: problem, success metric, measurement window | Before each build |
| Retrospective Note | Post-build learning: expected vs actual, what to change | At 30-day review for each automation |
| Monthly Automation Review | CEO/CTO alignment: health check, priorities, kills/expands | Monthly |
| Experiment Registry | Accumulated learning: what works at Cosuno | Quarterly summary |

**Why governance matters in this role:**
Without structure, internal automation becomes shadow IT — things running in prod that nobody owns, break silently, and can't be handed off. In a startup that's building AI-assisted products for customers, internal AI that's ungoverned is a credibility risk. The governance model here is lightweight by design — no committees, no approval chains — but it creates the paper trail that means this role compounds over time rather than accumulating technical debt.
