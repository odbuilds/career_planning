## What 9fin Does, Who They Serve, and What They're Building Toward

### The Problem They Solve

Debt capital markets — bonds, loans, leveraged finance, private credit — is the world's largest asset class at ~$145 trillion. Despite this, the infrastructure credit professionals work with is fragmented and antiquated: Bloomberg terminals for pricing, separate databases for covenants, PDFs emailed around for deal documents, manual research to compile comps. A credit analyst preparing a memo on a leveraged buyout might spend hours pulling data from five different sources, reading 80-page documents to find the three paragraphs that matter, and manually building a comparable company table that someone else on the desk built six months ago.

9fin's thesis: that problem is solvable with AI over proprietary structured data, and whoever solves it owns the workflow of every credit professional at every major institution.

### What They Actually Are

9fin is a data, news, and AI platform unified into a single workflow tool for credit teams. Three layers:

**1. Data and analytics**
20+ years of historical bond and loan data, covering 1,800+ issuers and 16,000+ financial instruments. Capital structures, covenant terms, financials, pricing history, and distress indicators — all structured and searchable. This is the proprietary data moat. The AI is meaningful because of what it runs on. A generic LLM can't analyse covenants because it doesn't have the structured covenant data to compare against. 9fin's AI is differentiated by the proprietary dataset, not the model.

## Tool Selection: n8n vs Agentic Frameworks vs Other Options

*This section is core to the role. "Evaluate and implement AI tools" is in the job description, and the interview will likely probe whether you can make and defend tool choices — not just list tools you've used. The right answer is never "the tool I know best" — it's "the tool whose trade-offs fit this problem, this team, and this risk profile." At a financial services company, security posture and failure mode matter as much as capability.*

---

### Why Tool Choice Has Downstream Consequences

A wrong tool choice doesn't just create technical debt — it creates **organisational debt**. The specific failure modes:

- **Build a reasoning task in a deterministic tool** (n8n for something that needs a real agent): you get a brittle flow that handles the cases you designed for and breaks silently on everything else. At 9fin, where a missed covenant or wrong extraction can be a risk event, silent failure is worse than no automation at all.
- **Build an operational workflow in code** (Python for something n8n would handle): you've created a single point of failure — yourself. Every time the workflow needs updating, someone needs you. In a team that's supposed to be scaling AI adoption, that's the opposite of the goal. It also means the business can't see what the automation is doing, which kills trust.
- **Use a hosted prototyping tool in production** (Flowise, Stack AI): you lose the ability to trace what's happening. When the output is wrong — and it will be — you can't see which step failed, what it received, or why. In a financial context where wrong = risk event, opacity is unacceptable.
- **Centralise credentials wrong**: credentials in .env files scattered across scripts mean: security risk (who has access?), operational risk (what happens when someone leaves?), and no audit trail. In financial services, audit trail is not optional.
- **Choose AWS-native infrastructure for fast iteration**: Step Functions and Bedrock are the right production layer for scale, but they have high iteration cost and no canvas. If you're in the exploratory phase, locking into AWS-native infrastructure before the problem is well-understood means you'll be rebuilding later.

The implication: **tool choice is a product decision, not just an engineering preference.** It determines who can own the thing you build, what happens when it breaks, how fast you can change it, and whether the organisation can see and trust it.

---

### The Core Decision Framework

Three questions before looking at any tool:

1. **Is the workflow deterministic or does it require reasoning?**
A sequence of fixed steps with branching logic is a fundamentally different problem from a task where a model needs to decide what to do next based on what it finds. Treating a reasoning problem as a deterministic workflow doesn't simplify it — it just hides the complexity until it breaks.

2. **Who needs to see it, operate it, and maintain it after you build it?**
The right engineer-only answer and the right shared-ownership answer are different tools. Building in Python when a business analyst needs to modify parameters or understand what's running makes you a permanent dependency on your own automation.

3. **What's the cost of failure — and is it visible or silent?**
A notification workflow that fails means someone doesn't get a message. A covenant extraction pipeline that fails means an analyst may act on wrong data. Those require different error tolerance, different alerting, and different levels of observability.

### n8n: When to Use It and Why It Matters

**Best for:** Deterministic, multi-step workflows connecting external services with clear triggers and fixed logic.

**Visual display — and why it matters beyond aesthetics:**
The n8n canvas is the documentation. For a company like 9fin building AI adoption across departments, this is not a nice-to-have — it's the difference between a workflow the business owns and one only you can explain. When a credit ops manager asks "why is my data enrichment behaving differently?", showing them the canvas takes two minutes. Walking them through Python takes forty, and they still won't be able to modify it themselves. Visual display also matters for trust: people are more likely to adopt and rely on something they can inspect. Opacity creates anxiety.

**Credential management — and the risk of getting it wrong:**
n8n stores all credentials in a centralised, encrypted store. You connect to Salesforce once; every workflow uses that connection. The alternative — credentials in .env files per script — means: no audit trail of which workflow uses which credential, security risk when team members change, and a support burden when credentials rotate. In a 400-person company with multiple engineers working on AI tooling, distributed credential management is an incident waiting to happen. Centralised management also makes offboarding clean — one place to rotate, one place to audit.

**Low iteration cost — and why it compounds:**
Modifying an n8n workflow doesn't require a deployment pipeline. At a company scaling AI adoption, the ability to fix a broken step or adjust a filter in five minutes (versus opening a PR, waiting for review, deploying) determines whether iteration actually happens. Tools with high change cost get left broken longer, which means less trust, which means less adoption.

**Where n8n breaks down — and what goes wrong:**
The n8n AI Agent node exists, but it's shallow: one LLM call, one routing decision. It can't loop, can't maintain state across multiple tool calls, and can't handle variable-length reasoning. If you force a reasoning task into n8n — say, "extract all covenants from this 40-page term sheet and flag the unusual ones" — you'll get a prompt that produces inconsistent output on edge cases with no way to trace why. The workflow will look fine on the canvas and fail silently on the 15% of documents that don't match your assumptions. That's the worst failure mode in financial services.

**9fin context for this role:** n8n is the right recommendation for internal operational workflows — CRM enrichment, notification routing, report generation, data sync across teams. It's also the right tool to recommend when the recipient isn't a developer: the visual canvas means the team can see what's running without needing you to explain it. For this role specifically, n8n is a tool Oliver would be *recommending and deploying*, not necessarily building from scratch — the distinction matters in the interview.

---

### Agentic Frameworks (LangGraph / LangChain): When to Use Them and Why

**Best for:** Tasks where a model must reason, decide what to do next, use tools, and handle outputs that vary in structure and length.

**Why genuine reasoning loops matter — and what you lose without them:**
LangGraph lets the model observe the result of each step and decide what to do next. In a document intelligence task — extract covenants, validate against known patterns, flag anomalies, request clarification on ambiguous terms — the number of steps and the path through them isn't fixed. Different documents take different paths. n8n can't represent that. What you get if you try is a fixed path that handles the common case and fails on everything else, with no way to gracefully handle the exceptions.

**State management — and why it matters for quality:**
LangGraph's state machine carries context across nodes. A covenant extraction agent can note what it found in section 3 and use that when interpreting section 7. An n8n workflow has no memory between nodes except what you explicitly pass. For financial documents where terms reference each other across sections, stateless processing means missed connections — which in a credit context is a quality failure.

**Evaluation integration — and why it's non-negotiable at 9fin:**
LangSmith traces map to individual LangGraph nodes. When an extraction is wrong, you can see exactly which node produced the wrong output, what it received as input, and what it returned. This is the observability that makes iterative improvement possible. Without it you're debugging in the dark. For a company whose entire value proposition is AI-quality-over-proprietary-data, not having node-level tracing on your AI pipelines is a gap — it means you can't systematically improve output quality, only react to reported failures.

**Production-grade deployment — and what it enables:**
Python means the full engineering ecosystem: unit tests, CI/CD, type checking, versioning, code review. An operational workflow doesn't need this. A pipeline that extracts and analyses financial documents and feeds analyst workflows does. This is the difference between something you run and something you trust.

**Where LangGraph breaks down — and the organisational consequence:**
No visual canvas means no non-technical oversight. A business analyst cannot inspect a LangGraph graph, understand what it does, or modify it without engineering help. If you build everything in LangGraph, you've centralised all operational knowledge in the engineering team. That's fine for product pipelines with clear ownership — it's the wrong choice for internal tooling that should be broadly accessible and modifiable.

**Credential management in code — and the discipline it requires:**
LangGraph has no credential store. That means explicit secrets management: environment variables, AWS Secrets Manager, or similar. This is more engineering overhead and requires the team to have clear conventions. If those conventions slip, you get credentials in code — which at a financial services company isn't just bad practice, it's a compliance risk.

**9fin context for this role:** LangGraph is the right recommendation when an internal team needs a reasoning pipeline — for example, if the editorial team wants an agent that researches a credit and produces a structured briefing across multiple sources. It's also the honest answer when someone asks "why not just use the n8n AI node?" — the agent node can't loop or maintain state, and for document-heavy financial workflows that matters. Knowing this distinction is what separates a credible tool recommendation from a naive one.

### Other Options and Their Implications

**Direct Python scripts (no framework)**
Right for: single-purpose, low-frequency batch jobs one engineer owns. A nightly data sync is a script, not a product. Don't over-engineer it.

Wrong for — and why it matters: anything a team beyond you needs to monitor or modify. A Python script in production with no owner convention is a support risk: nobody knows it's running, nobody knows what breaks it, nobody knows how to change it. The first time it fails silently and someone makes a decision on stale data, it gets blamed on "the AI". That sets back adoption.

**Hosted AI pipeline tools (Langflow, Flowise, Stack AI, Dify)**
Right for: rapid prototyping before you know what you're building. Useful for demonstrating what's possible to stakeholders quickly.

Wrong for production — and why: these tools abstract away the pipeline implementation. The benefit (fast to build) becomes the liability (can't trace failures). When a production pipeline produces a wrong output, you need to know exactly which node failed and why. Hosted tools make that hard or impossible. For 9fin, where wrong output can feed credit decisions, that opacity is a disqualifying constraint.

Live reference: evaluated Stack AI, Langflow, and OpenAI GPTs against LangChain for the Canadian telecom client. The finding was that hosted tools were faster to demo but consistently hit walls on observability and customisation in production-grade evaluation.

**AWS-native (Step Functions, Bedrock, Lambda)**
Right for: when the pipeline is proven, needs to scale, and the team has the infra expertise. This is the production deployment layer — not the iteration layer.

The implication of choosing it too early: AWS infrastructure has high change cost. If you lock a pipeline into Step Functions before the design is stable, you're paying deployment overhead on every iteration. At the exploratory phase, that overhead kills the fast feedback cycle you need to find out what actually works.

Right relationship to n8n/LangGraph: AWS is where things go when they're ready to run at scale with full infra reliability. n8n and LangGraph are where things get built and proven. They should co-exist, not compete. 9fin's JD explicitly asks for AWS knowledge because their production ML infrastructure is almost certainly there — the question is how the new AI layer integrates with it.

**MCP (Model Context Protocol)**
Right for: standardising how an LLM agent accesses external tools and data sources. Not a pipeline tool — a protocol for tool calling within an agent.

The implication: if 9fin is building agents that need structured access to multiple internal data sources (proprietary bond data, filing databases, Bloomberg), MCP is the right architectural pattern for defining those tools. It means the tool interface is standardised and reusable across agents, rather than custom API calls baked into each pipeline. This matters for maintenance — when a data source changes, you update the MCP tool, not every pipeline that uses it.

### How to Frame This in the Interview

**PCR answer — the version that shows architectural judgment, not just tool knowledge:**

"My starting point is always two questions: is this a deterministic workflow or a reasoning task, and who needs to own it after I build it? Those two questions almost always determine the tool.

For deterministic workflows — data enrichment, notifications, CRM updates — n8n is the right answer. Not because of the connectors, but because the visual canvas means the business can see what's running and a non-engineer can modify it. If I build that in Python, I become a permanent dependency on my own automation. Every change goes through me, the business can't audit it, and the first time it breaks silently, trust in the whole AI programme takes a hit. Credential management matters too: centralised storage with an audit trail is a basic hygiene requirement in financial services that scattered .env files can't provide.

For reasoning tasks — extraction from financial documents, multi-hop research, QA loops — n8n can't actually do the job. The AI Agent node gives you one LLM call and a routing decision; it can't loop, maintain state, or decide what to do based on what it finds. Forcing a covenant extraction pipeline into n8n means it handles the 85% of documents that look like your test cases and fails silently on the rest. That failure mode is worse than no automation in a credit context. LangGraph with LangSmith tracing is the right answer — you get the reasoning loop and the node-level observability to actually improve quality over time.

The other piece that's relevant for 9fin specifically: AWS is almost certainly your production infrastructure. The question I'd want to understand is how the new AI layer integrates with that — n8n and LangGraph should be the build and iteration layer, with proven pipelines migrating to AWS-native infrastructure when they're stable and need scale. They're not competing choices; they're different phases of the same lifecycle."
