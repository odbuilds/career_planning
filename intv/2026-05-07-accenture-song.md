## Section 13: Common Agentic App Failures (Practitioner View)

*What actually goes wrong — sourced from engineers and practitioners, not vendor marketing. Use this to sound credible when asked about failure modes, risk, or what you've learned.*

---

**1. Building an agent when a simpler solution would have worked**
The most common failure isn't a technical one — it's a scoping one. Teams reach for agentic architecture because it sounds right, then spend months debugging non-deterministic behaviour that a well-structured chain or a simple prompt would have handled cleanly. The overhead of autonomous reasoning compounds failure risk across every step. Practitioners are now explicit about this: the decision to build an agent versus a chain versus a single prompt should be made on task structure, not ambition. If the routing is knowable in advance, you don't need an agent.

*Relevance to this role:* Being conservative about when to use agentic architecture is a sign of experience, not timidity. Oliver can use the LinkedIn Post Agent as a concrete example — it looks like an agent but uses deterministic routing, which makes it testable and auditable.

---

**2. Scaling POC architecture directly to production**
Prototypes are built for speed — they prioritise demonstrating capability over clean decomposition. The failure pattern is treating the demo code as a foundation and trying to "polish" it for production. Tangled prototype logic doesn't just create technical debt — it directly degrades model reasoning and tool selection, because LLMs are sensitive to the quality of the context and instructions they receive. The architecture itself becomes a prompt quality problem.

*Klarna example:* Klarna tried to replace 700 customer service agents with AI too quickly, and customer satisfaction dropped on emotionally complex interactions. They had to rebuild human capacity back into the model as a hybrid — an expensive lesson about the gap between what works in a demo and what works at scale.

---

**3. Underspecified tool integrations**
This is the failure mode that catches experienced engineers. The agent works perfectly on the happy path, then breaks in production because of API pagination it wasn't designed for, rate limits it never hit in testing, missing sort parameters, or strict search constraints that don't exist in the dev environment. Tool definitions fundamentally shape how the model reasons — a vague or incomplete tool contract causes the agent to hallucinate workarounds. The fix is treating every external service contract as a first-class design artifact, not a downstream integration task.

---

**4. No observability from day one**
The practitioner quote that circulates on this: *"When things go wrong, no one can say why."* Without execution tracing and structured logging across every LLM call and tool invocation, debugging production failures is guesswork. The failure mode is silent and slow — the agent doesn't crash, it just produces wrong outputs that take days to trace. Practitioners now treat observability infrastructure (LangSmith or equivalent) as a prerequisite before first live run, not something added after a problem surfaces.

---

**5. No regression testing framework**
AI agent behaviour changes between model versions, prompt iterations, and tool updates — often in ways that break existing functionality while improving the specific thing you were working on. Without automated test datasets covering the full range of expected inputs, teams lose the ability to detect these regressions. Manual testing at agent scale is not feasible. The practitioners who ship reliably build test suites before they build features.

---

**6. All-at-once rollout**
Single large deployments hide which component caused a failure. The pattern that works is shadow mode first (agent runs alongside humans, outputs compared but not acted on), then narrow live rollout, then expansion. The Klarna case is again instructive: the bot handled routine volume competently but failed on the emotionally complex interactions that are statistically rare but commercially critical. An incremental rollout would have surfaced that before it became a public story.

---

**7. Governance and human oversight added as an afterthought**
Agents that take real-world actions — sending emails, updating records, triggering payments — need human-in-the-loop checkpoints designed in from the start, not bolted on after a near-miss. The failure pattern: the agent works correctly 97% of the time, and the 3% failure rate is acceptable in isolation, but the actions are irreversible and the 3% cases are the ones that create legal or reputational exposure. Responsible AI is an architecture decision, not a compliance layer.

## Section 14: Why Companies Get Stuck in POC

*The pattern is consistent enough that practitioners now have a name for it: the pilot-to-production gap. Understanding why it happens is useful both for the interview (it's a real client problem Accenture Song faces) and for positioning Oliver's evaluation and enablement work as the thing that closes it.*

---

**The headline:** More than 80% of AI projects fail to deliver meaningful production value — twice the failure rate of traditional IT projects. S&P Global's 2025 survey found 42% of companies abandoned most AI initiatives that year, more than double the 17% the year before. Gartner projects over 40% of agentic AI projects will be cancelled by end of 2027.

**The cause is almost never the model.** The NTT Data consultant quote that's been circulating: *"The model is rarely the main problem."* The failure cluster is organisational and architectural, not technical.

---

**Root cause 1: The demo data isn't the production data**
A POC runs on curated data — clean, consistent, scoped. Production data is fragmented across multiple systems, uses different definitions of basic terms ("customer," "order," "revenue") between departments, has gaps and formatting mismatches, and bears almost no resemblance to the sample dataset that powered the demo. The agent breaks not because the AI is wrong but because the inputs it was never designed for.

**Root cause 2: Integration was treated as a downstream problem**
Pilots work in controlled environments with narrow scope. Moving to production requires connecting to systems that weren't designed with AI in mind — legacy databases, third-party APIs, internal tools with no proper contracts. The integration layer (the infrastructure that connects systems, moves data reliably, and lets automated actions trigger across tools) is the thing that kills projects when it's treated as a later problem rather than a design constraint from day one.

**Root cause 3: No clear business ownership after the demo**
The pattern: budgets get approved, pilots get built, demos get applauded, and then nothing ships. The reason is usually that the pilot was owned by a technical champion who doesn't have the authority to drive change across the operational teams that would need to change their workflows. Without a business sponsor who owns the outcome — not just the experiment — the project stalls at handover.

**Root cause 4: Success was never defined**
"Improve productivity" or "reduce costs" are not success criteria. They're aspirations. When there's no specific, measurable definition of what production success looks like — what metric moves, by how much, by when — there's no way to know if the project is working, no way to justify continued investment, and no trigger for escalating when it isn't working. The POC gets extended indefinitely because nobody can declare it a failure or a success.

**Root cause 5: Real workflows are judgment-heavy, not rule-based**
Most of the workflows where agentic AI can add value aren't clean processes — they're driven by implicit expertise, contextual judgment, and years of institutional knowledge held by specific people. Capturing that logic, mapping the decision points, and building agents that can handle the full range of edge cases requires structured discovery work before any prototyping starts. Projects that skip that work build agents for the idealised version of the workflow, not the real one.

**Root cause 6: Governance and compliance weren't in the room during scoping**
Security review, data residency requirements, audit trail needs, and approval workflows are real constraints that only appear late if the people who own them weren't involved early. A technically excellent agent that can't pass security review never ships. This is especially acute for enterprise clients in regulated industries — financial services, insurance, healthcare — where Oliver has spent his career.

---

**How Oliver closes the POC gap (the positioning angle)**
The evaluation frameworks, human baseline processes, and enablement programmes Oliver builds are specifically designed to address root causes 1, 3, and 4: validating that the system works against real data before handover, ensuring client teams can own and improve it without ongoing dependency, and providing a measurable quality benchmark that defines success in operational terms. This is the thing to say if asked: *"The work I do is specifically designed so the project doesn't stop at the POC."*

## Section 15: Using Workshops to Define Goals and Specs

*How structured discovery and scoping workshops prevent the failures in Sections 13 and 14 — and how Oliver's approach connects to established methodology including CDI's framework.*

---

**Why workshops before prototyping**
The practitioner consensus is clear: spec-driven development produces better AI projects than build-first approaches. A good spec defines six things before prototyping starts: outcomes, scope boundaries, constraints, prior decisions, task breakdown, and verification criteria. Workshops are the fastest way to surface all six in a room with the people who hold the relevant knowledge — business stakeholders, operational teams, technical architects, and compliance leads.

The workshop isn't a requirements-gathering session in the traditional sense. It's a structured process for converting implicit, fragmented knowledge into explicit, shared design decisions. The goal is not a document — it's alignment that prevents the three most common derailments: building the wrong thing, building it for a context that doesn't exist in production, and handing it to a team that can't maintain it.

---

**CDI's framework (where Oliver's practice methodology is grounded)**
The Conversation Design Institute's Standards Framework — which forms the methodological backbone of CDI Services, where Oliver worked — structures the pre-build discovery phase around three inputs:

- **User data analysis:** Demographics, behavioural patterns, and conversation data (call logs, transcripts) to identify where automation actually adds value vs. where it creates friction
- **Operational data review:** Existing process maps and system integrations to understand what constraints the solution has to survive
- **Stakeholder alignment:** Clear definition of business goals, success metrics, and organisational ownership before any design work begins

CDI's assessment methodology uses what they call the "integral strategy model" — a structured review across multiple dimensions to ensure the AI solution aligns with business objectives, not just technical requirements. The discovery output is a business case with specific, measurable objectives tied to the broader strategy, plus a design brief that the build team can actually execute against.

The key principle CDI emphasises: *"Setting specific, measurable objectives ensures that AI efforts align with the broader business strategy"* — and this is established before design, not derived from it.

---

**Workshop structure that works in practice**
Based on Oliver's delivery experience, the discovery workshop sequence that reliably produces a scoping brief a build team can use:

**Session 1 — Problem definition (business stakeholders only)**
What's the business problem? What's the cost of it not being solved? What does success look like in 6 months, measured how? What are the non-negotiables (regulatory, operational, political)? The output is a problem statement and success criteria, not a solution brief.

**Session 2 — Current state mapping (operational teams)**
How does the workflow actually work today — not the documented version, the real version? Where does it break, slow down, or require judgment calls? What data exists, where does it live, and who owns it? This surfaces the integration constraints and the implicit expertise that needs to be encoded.

**Session 3 — Scope definition (technical + business together)**
What's in scope for the first phase? What's explicitly deferred? What are the failure modes that would be unacceptable (data exposure, incorrect decisions in regulated contexts, customer-facing errors)? What does the human-in-the-loop model look like? The output is a design brief with explicit scope boundaries.

**Session 4 — Evaluation design (before prototyping)**
What does "working" look like? What's the test set? Who signs off on quality? This is the step most workshops skip — defining evaluation criteria before the build creates a quality bar that the prototype has to meet, rather than a post-hoc judgement of whether the demo looked good.

---

**How this connects to the Accenture Song context**
Accenture Song's clients are enterprises being told they need an agentic commerce strategy — but most of them don't have a clear problem definition, a scoped first use case, or any sense of what production success looks like. The workshop capability is what converts a vague mandate ("we need to do something with AI agents") into a deliverable brief that a team can actually build against. Oliver's combination of structured discovery methodology (from CDI practice) and hands-on build capability (from HumanFirst delivery) is what allows him to run the workshop and then prototype the output — without the handoff failure that happens when strategy and delivery are separate tracks.
