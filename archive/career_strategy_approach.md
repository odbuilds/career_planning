# Understanding What I Mean by Career Strategy

*A facilitated discovery process — March 2026*

---

## The Problem with Starting with a Framework

Most career strategy advice hands you a framework first — "identify your target role, build the skills, create the portfolio." The problem: the framework assumes you already know what you want from your career, and just need help getting there.

But the harder question — the one that determines whether any plan is worth following — is: *what do I actually mean when I say I want a career strategy?* That is a personal question, not a professional one. It cannot be answered by a framework.

This document is not the strategy. It is the preparation for finding out what the strategy needs to be.

---

## What We Are Trying to Discover

Before we make any plan, we need to understand what you are actually trying to solve. "I want a career strategy" can mean many different things:

- I want clarity on *direction* — I don't know where I'm heading and it's bothering me
- I want *validation* — I think I know what I want but I'm not sure it's the right call
- I want a *plan* — I know what I want but don't know how to get there
- I want to *earn more* — the goal is financial, not directional
- I want to *feel more purposeful* — the goal is meaning, not money or status
- I want to *get out of something* — the strategy is as much about what to leave as what to build
- I want to *position myself for what's coming* — the goal is to not be left behind

These lead to very different conversations. We need to know which one — or which combination — is true for you.

---

## How We Will Find Out

### Step 1: Start with the feeling, not the goal

Before talking about roles, skills, or markets, we start with a simpler question: **what prompted this?**

Not "what do you want to achieve" — that is already too polished. What is the actual feeling or situation that made career strategy feel necessary right now? Dissatisfaction, anxiety, an opportunity, a conversation, a birthday — whatever it is.

The answer to that question tells us more about what kind of strategy you need than any skills audit.

### Step 2: Separate what you want from what you think you should want

Career planning conversations are full of "should" thinking — roles that sound impressive, skills that are in demand, income benchmarks, status signals. None of that is wrong, but it often drowns out what someone actually wants.

We will do a simple exercise: for each thing that comes up as a "career goal," we will ask *why* — not once, but repeatedly — until we get to the thing underneath. The goal is to find whether you are chasing an outcome you want, or a signal of an outcome you think you should want.

### Step 3: Understand what "good" looks like in practice

Abstract goals (be recognised as an expert, have more impact, earn well) are hard to plan around because they do not connect to daily experience. We need to understand what *a good working week* actually looks like for you — concretely:

- What kind of work do you want to be doing day to day?
- Who do you want to be working with and for?
- What do you want to *not* be doing?
- Where do you want to be doing it — employed, independent, building something?

These are not strategy questions. They are preference questions. But they constrain the strategy entirely.

### Step 4: Identify what "strategy" means vs. what "tactics" means for you

Some people use "career strategy" when they mean "I need to be more intentional about my choices." Others mean "I need a specific 12-month plan with milestones." Others mean "I need to know my positioning so I can communicate it clearly."

We need to find out what level of clarity and structure would actually feel useful to you — because a highly detailed plan can feel constraining for one person and grounding for another.

---

## The Questions We Will Work Through

These are not a questionnaire to fill in. They are the territory we will explore in conversation, in whatever order makes sense as it unfolds.

**On the motivation:**
- What made you want to think about this now, rather than six months ago or six months from now?
- If nothing changed in the next year, what would that feel like?

**On what you want:**
- What would you be doing if career concerns were not a factor — if money, status, and other people's opinions were irrelevant?
- What parts of the work you do now do you want *more* of?
- What are you hoping a career strategy will give you that you don't currently have?

**On what you don't want:**
- What are you trying to move away from, not just toward?
- What would a bad outcome look like in five years?

**On how you define success:**
- How will you know when the career strategy has worked?
- Is the definition of success yours, or is it influenced by how it will look to others?

**On what "strategy" means to you:**
- Do you want a direction, a plan, or a position — or something else?
- How much structure is useful to you vs. how much feels constraining?
- What have you tried before when thinking about this, and what was missing from it?

---

## What We Will Produce

The output of this process is not a career plan. It is a document that captures:

1. **What you are actually trying to solve** — stated in your own words, not in career-advice language
2. **What success looks like to you** — concretely, not abstractly
3. **What constraints are real** — financial, geographic, relational, timing
4. **What "career strategy" means in your specific case** — direction, plan, positioning, something else

Once we have that, we can decide what kind of strategy work is actually needed — and do only that, rather than building a generic framework neither of us will use.

---

## Where We Start

A single question to answer first, in as much or as little detail as feels right:

**What made you want to think about career strategy now?**
